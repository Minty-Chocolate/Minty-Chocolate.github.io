## The Issue with Timing

In the framework of the Barra factor model, standard risk factors for equity cross-sectional strategies typically comprise Market, Industry, and Style. Portfolio 
managers (PMs) conventionally seek neutral exposure to these risk factors to isolate and maximize pure alpha.

In practice, however, the concept of timing—whether framed as Market Timing, Industry Timing, or Style/Factor Timing—has never ceased to captivate both academia 
and practitioners. Yet timing is widely castigated as an investment sin—most notably by Cliff Asness, albeit primarily directed at style timing. The case for factor 
timing has been further undermined by a deluge of underwhelming research. Much of the literature suffers from three pervasive pitfalls: lacking clear economic 
intuition, relying on ad-hoc discretion rather than systematic discipline, or suffering from severe in-sample overfitting given the scarcity of independent 
macro/market cycles.

Perhaps the most formidable headwind, however, is the benchmark itself. Beating—or even adding a viable overlay to—a simple Buy & Hold strategy has proven
exceptionally difficult, particularly against the backdrop of the historical bull market run that defined the post-2010 era.

## The Underlying Driving Force


Despite these hurdles, timing is far from a dead end. To begin with, style factors vary widely in effective horizons and encompass disparate underlying mechanisms, 
making style timing exceptionally complex—a difficulty further underscored by recent literature employing the latest shiny ML methods to tame the “factor zoo.” 
Market timing remains equally intriguing, though empirical results are mixed: fundamental signals offer limited predictive power even over longer horizons, 
while flow data, though highly informative, is largely proprietary and difficult to source.

Industry timing presents its own dynamics. While pure cross-sectional alpha within industries is scarce, institutional flows heavily dictate cross-industry 
performance—pricing in earnings revisions and multiple re-ratings much like broader macro asset classes. Furthermore, discretionary hedge funds that bypass 
rigid cross-industry market neutrality to express thematic industry tilts often generate meaningful divergence in both excess returns and risk relative to 
their purely sector-neutral peers. In an increasingly crowded market dominated by macro themes like AI, industry timing emerges as a compelling avenue: 
it provides aggregated exposure, a broader effective opportunity set, and deep liquidity.


# Strategy Backtests

I recently come upon a strategy that may be actually useful. The strategy outperforms the benchmark unlike most. However, there are discretionary selections as to 
what are admitted into the universe and how to construct the signal; I also picked one of the most promising results, so it is not entirely overfitting-free. 

The universe selection is the most suspicious of overfitting. To convince myself of that it is actually a real phenomenon, I conducted leave-one-out on the selected universe. 
The faintly shaded lines are what would the strategy cumulative returns be if a particular underlying instrument is dropped. The blue line is a sort of "synthesis" 
(let's call it that) of the signals from all the leave-one-out strategies. The cyan line is the SPY cumulative returns. The red line is the strategy cumulative returns.
It seems that either the universe is working in locksteps since the strategy are better than leave-on-out versions. 

![](/images/writing/industry-trend-reversal/pic1.png)

It also matters to analyze if it is just market exposure with more leverage, or just a timed market exposure, since the portfolio will adjust notional and leverage 
through time. Regression results show that the alpha is there when taking out market component, not so much when taking out both market and timed market component, 
in terms of t stats. But the magnitude of alpha is very similar. In sample results show that combining them with benchmark in an optimal fashion can boost sharpe
from ~0.94 to ~1.1, which is a modest boost. According to sum-of-squares formula for Sharpe, $$SR^2_{\text{total}} = \sum SR^2_i$$, the increase $1-0.94^2/1.1^2$ is 
approximately 25%. The return is a more interesting figure here. But given that the strategy makes concentrated bets, it may appeal to retail investors (It is designed
as a CTA-style low-frequency strategy).

## Maybe This Works?


Maybe the strategy is working, or, it could be that I'm good at overfitting and tuning. But whatever the case, it should just be taken as proof-of-concept. 
This strategy is not for hedge funds, since they operate on a much higher frequency. While it may be useful for small investors with longer horizons looking 
to tune their leverage and fare than the index. I am also not sure that the phenomenon I discovered is unfortunately also a product of overfitting or 
manual rules. Like all strategies, the only way to tell if it works is to continuously moniter the performance.
